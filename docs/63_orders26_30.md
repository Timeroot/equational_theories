# E63 at orders 26 and 30: construction barriers

**Status, 2026-09-24: both orders remain open.** This pass found no model and
does not prove nonexistence at either order. It establishes several obstacles
to construction, including complete exclusions of some natural construction
classes. Nothing here has been formalized in Lean or added to the website's
proved spectrum.

We use the finite E229 presentation `(y*(y*x))*y=x`, which has the same finite
spectrum as E63. Write `L_y(x)=y*x` and `R_y(x)=x*y`. Then `R_y=L_y⁻²`.
Left translations have no 2-cycles, and the cycle containing y under L_y
has length 1 or 3. These facts and the conversion to E63 are explained in
[the main progress note](63_spectrum_progress.md).

## 1. General restrictions

An order-26 E229 model must be noncommutative. In a commutative model every
left translation has cube equal to the identity, so at order 26 each has
at least two fixed points. Yet the fixed-point counts sum to 26, since each
column x contains the value x exactly once. This does not exclude a
commutative order-30 model.

Neither target can have a constant square map. If `x*x=e` for every x, then
e is idempotent and every x commutes with e, since an element commutes with
its square. Thus `L_e³=id`. Its only fixed point is e: if `ex=xe=x`, E229
applied with centre x and input e gives `(xx)x=e`, hence `ex=e`, so x=e.
Consequently all other points lie in 3-cycles, and the order is 1 modulo 3.

Neither target can have a two-sided identity. In an E229 loop with identity e,
E229 gives `(xx)x=e`, and commutation with the square gives `x(xx)=e`.
Writing s=xx and applying the same calculation to s gives `(ss)s=e=xs`,
so `ss=x` by cancellation. No x other than e has `xx=x`. Squaring therefore
pairs the nonidentity elements, and the loop has odd order.

For any proper subquasigroup P of size p, the order is at least 3p. For y
outside P, the sets P, L_y(P), and L_y²(P) are pairwise disjoint. The last
image is R_y⁻¹(P); Latin cancellation puts both images outside P, and
injectivity of L_y separates them. If y is idempotent, it belongs to none
of these sets, giving the stronger bound `n ≥ 3p+1`.

## 2. No standard singular product can produce either order

The singular product has order `n=v(q−p)+p`, where V is an idempotent E229
quasigroup of order v>1, Q contains a subquasigroup P of order p>0, and an
E229 model of order q−p supplies the other factor.

The smallest nontrivial idempotent E229 order is 5. At order 3, a left
translation would have to fix its centre and swap the other two points,
which is impossible. At order 4, its other three points form a 3-cycle,
making the operation commutative; a commutative idempotent Latin square
cannot have even order, since each symbol has an odd number of off-diagonal
occurrences but symmetry pairs those occurrences. Order 2 is already excluded.

Since `q ≥ 3p`, we get `n ≥ (2v+1)p`. For n=26 or 30 and v≥5 this gives p≤2.
There is no order-2 subquasigroup, so p=1. For 26, `v(q−1)=25` leaves only
`(v,q)=(5,6)` or `(25,2)`, both impossible. For 30, `v(q−1)=29` leaves
`(v,q)=(29,2)`, also impossible. This excludes the entire nontrivial singular
product construction, not just the factor models currently in our catalogue.

Ordinary direct products also fail: 26 has a factor 2, and every nontrivial
factorization of 30 uses an excluded order 2, 6, or 10. The exclusion at 10
is computational and has not been formalized in Lean.

## 3. No ordinary GDD construction, even with a common subquasigroup

Here a group-divisible design (GDD) partitions the points into groups, and
its transversal blocks cover each pair from different groups exactly once.
Fill each transversal block with an idempotent E229 model and each group
with an arbitrary E229 model. Every nontrivial block has size at least 5.

Two elementary counting bounds are useful. If a group has size g and x is
outside it, its g pairs with x require g distinct blocks through x, each
using at least four neighbours of x. Thus `v−g_x ≥ 4g`, where v is the
number of design points and g_x is x's group size. If B is a block of size k
and x is outside B, each other block through x meets B in at most one point.
Thus `v−g_x ≥ 4(k−1)`, and the bound is `4k` if x's group misses B.

**Without a common hole**, take v=26 or 30. There can be no block of size
at least 8. For v=26 the second bound is already impossible. For v=30,
an outside point in a singleton group requires 32 neighbours; an outside
point in a group of size at least 3 requires at least 28 neighbours but
has at most 27. A group of size 2 cannot be filled. Blocks therefore have
sizes 5 or 7 (there is no order-6 model). Consequently every group size is
even, since `v−g_x` is a sum of 4s and 6s. The first bound makes every
group size at most 7, so every group must have size 4. This contradicts
v=26 or 30.

**With one common point**, v is 25 or 29 and groups of size g require
fillings of order g+1. The same second bound excludes blocks of size at
least 8, even allowing 2-point groups. Blocks have sizes 5 or 7, so all
group sizes are odd. Sizes 1 and 5 are forbidden by the exclusions at
2 and 6. Every group has size at least 3, and the first bound now makes
every group size at most 6. All groups therefore have size 3, which cannot
sum to 25 or 29.

**With a common subquasigroup of size p≥3**, each group has size at least 2p
by the subquasigroup bound. At least five groups are needed for transversal
blocks, so the final order is at least 11p≥33. A common subquasigroup of
size 2 is impossible. These arguments exclude all of the stated ordinary
GDD constructions, regardless of which additional factor models exist.

They do not exclude more general partial-table blocks, such as Bennett's
incomplete table of type `2^4`.

## 4. Restrictions on partial-table constructions

Use the definition of a partitioned incomplete E229 Latin square from
[the order-158 note](63_order158_search.md): products within a hole are
undefined; each other row/column permutes the complement of its hole;
E229 holds on pairs from different holes.

For distinct holes of sizes h_i and h_j, necessarily

\[
  n \ge 3h_i+h_j.
\]

Indeed, for y in the j-th hole, that hole and the three disjoint sets
`H_i, L_y(H_i), L_y²(H_i)` are disjoint. This is stronger than applying the
ordinary subquasigroup bound to the largest hole alone.

**A partial E229 table with exactly four holes has four equal, even hole
sizes.** For centre y in one hole, take x in another. The three successive
points `x, L_y(x), L_y²(x)` occupy three distinct holes: the final identity
`L_y²(x)*y=x` excludes the hole of x for L_y²(x). Hence the hole labels in
every cycle of L_y repeat in an oriented cycle of the other three holes.
Those three holes have equal sizes. Varying y makes all four sizes equal,
say h. This also shows that fewer than four nonempty holes are impossible.

For evenness, let N_ijk count products with first argument in hole i, second
argument in hole j, and output in hole k. Along each of the cycles just
described, L_y and L_y⁻² have the same counts of transitions between hole
labels. Thus N_ijk=N_jik. Set N_012=a. The oriented cycles around hole 0 give
`N_023=N_031=a` and `N_021=h²−a`. Around hole 1, `N_102=a` gives
`N_123=a`. Around hole 2, `N_201=h²−a` gives `N_213=h²−a`.
Symmetry now gives `a=h²−a`. Therefore h is even.

Two particularly useful small ingredients were also excluded computationally:
a single-hole partial table of order 6 with hole size 2, and one of order 8
with hole size 2. The searches imposed **no outside idempotence**. Their
UNSAT traces were independently checked by DRAT-trim; they are not Lean proofs.

The order-6 partial exclusion also has the following paper proof. Let the
hole be {a,b}, with four other points W, and put `α=L_a|W`, `β=L_b|W`.
Neither permutation has a 2-cycle. Latinness requires both α,β and
α⁻²,β⁻² to disagree at every point. The available cycle types on four points
are `1^4`, `1·3`, and `4`. A four-point permutation check leaves only
`(1^4,4)` (up to exchange) or `(1·3,1·3)` with distinct fixed points.
For completeness, fixing a 4-cycle, the only other disjoint 4-cycle is its
inverse, which has the same square; a disjoint `1·3` permutation agrees
with that square at one point. An identity permutation cannot be disjoint
from a permutation with a fixed point.

In the first case a acts as an identity on W. Squaring on W is therefore a
fixed-point-free involution: `s(x)=xx` lies in W, `x s(x)=s(x)x=a`, and
`s(s(x))=x` by cancellation. Its value cannot equal x, β(x), or β²(x):
these are already supplied respectively by `x*a`, `b*x`, and `x*b`.
It must equal β³(x), which is a 4-cycle, a contradiction.

In the second case let u and v be the distinct fixed points of α and β.
Both a and b commute with W. A commuting pair also commutes with its
product, by `R_x=L_x⁻²` on the corresponding 1- or 3-cycle. Thus the
three points in W other than u commute pairwise, and likewise those other
than v. Only u,v could fail to commute; comparing the Latin row and column
at u forces them to commute too. The entire defined partial table is now
commutative. Each of its four full rows has a multiple of three fixed
points, whereas each hole row has one. There are exactly four fixed points
counted across all rows: each column indexed by W contributes one and
neither hole column contributes any. This gives `4=2+3k`, a contradiction.

These close the immediate variant that allows the last group filling to
replace the common hole's multiplication. For a 2-point common hole, the
first groups have size at least 6, using the order-6 partial-table exclusion.
At 26 there is not room for four such groups and a nonempty last group.
At 30, parity and the order-6 exclusion leave five groups of sizes
`8,6,6,6,2`. A 5-GDD on exactly five groups would require equal group sizes.
Larger common holes are excluded by the size bound and parity as above.

The same small exclusions also block doubling a 4-GDD with the `2^4` partial
table. Without common points, the base group sizes must be even, contradicting
base order 13 or 15. With two common points, order 26 reduces exactly to a
4-GDD of type `3^4` followed by three partial order-8 fillings with 2-holes;
the latter do not exist. At order 30, the necessary group congruences already
contradict the order-6 partial-table exclusion. With four common points, the
only remaining size candidate is `4,4,4,1` at base order 13, impossible for
a 4-GDD on four groups. Larger common holes exceed the available size.

## 5. A cyclic construction reduced to a small permutation problem

Suppose an automorphism is one q-cycle, labeled by Z/qZ, and fixes a set P
pointwise. For each e in P there is a shift a_e with

\[
  e*x=x+a_e,\qquad x*e=x-2a_e\quad(x\in\mathbb Z/q\mathbb Z).
\]

The a_e are distinct, and so are the -2a_e. Let d_e be the unique difference
with `x*(x+d_e)=e`. Away from these differences, write
`x*(x+t)=x+F(t)`. Put A={a_e} and D={d_e}. Latinness makes F a bijection
from `Z/qZ \ D` to `Z/qZ \ (-2A)` and makes `t ↦ F(t)−t` a bijection
onto `Z/qZ \ A`. E229 gives the three essential relations

\[
 F(a_e)=d_e,\qquad F(-2a_e)=-d_e,\qquad
 F(2a_e)=d_e+2a_e.
\]

In particular D misses `A ∪ 2A ∪ (-2A)`. These relations concern no product
between two fixed points, so any E229 filling of P can be used afterward.

For odd q, A cannot contain 0: the displayed equations would force d_e=0,
contrary to D missing A. Also A has no opposite pair. More generally, if
`2a=-2b`, the equations force `4a=0`. Thus for odd q the sets 2A and -2A
are disjoint, giving `q ≥ 3|P|`. If q is not divisible by 3, D cannot contain
0 either, and `q ≥ 3|P|+1`. At even q, the same calculation bounds the
overlap of 2A and -2A by the two-torsion of the subgroup 2Z/qZ. In particular
`|P| ≤ (q+2)/4` for q=2 modulo 4, and `|P| ≤ q/4+1` for q divisible by 4.

For order 26 this immediately excludes a 19-cycle with seven fixed points
and an 18-cycle with eight fixed points. For order 30 it excludes a 22-cycle
with eight fixed points, a 21-cycle with nine fixed points, and a 20-cycle
with ten fixed points.

### A paper exclusion of the 23-cycle at order 30

Here q=23 and |P|=7. The sets 2A and -2A use 14 nonzero residues, while
D needs seven more. Thus A has at most one point outside `2A ∪ (-2A)`.
Modulo sign, multiplication by 2 cycles through all eleven classes of
nonzero residues. A's seven classes must form one consecutive interval in
that cycle. Rescale so

\[
 a_i=\epsilon_i2^i\ (0\le i\le6),\qquad \epsilon_0=1.
\]

Then, independent of the remaining signs,
`D={3,6,11,12,17,20,22}`. The displayed F equations imply

\[
 d_i=\begin{cases}
 d_{i-1}+a_i&\epsilon_i=\epsilon_{i-1},\\
 -d_{i-1}&\epsilon_i=-\epsilon_{i-1}.
 \end{cases}
\]

The d_i must be distinct members of D. After two steps the only prefixes
are `(20,22,3)` with signs `+++`, and `(20,3,22)` with signs `+--`.
The next step eliminates the second and extends the first to `(20,22,3,11)`.
The next step is forced to give `(20,22,3,11,12)` with last sign negative.
At the fifth step both choices repeat an earlier value: 3 or 11.
Contradiction. This argument excludes the specified automorphism without
an UNSAT oracle.

### Remaining cyclic templates

The [portable generator](../scripts/spectrum_63_cyclic.py) implements the
small permutation problem above. It enumerates shift sets modulo multiplication
by units of Z/qZ and permutation of the fixed points. It searches one cyclic
row, not an arbitrary multiplication table. Every positive output is expanded
and checked against all E229 and E63 instances.

The remaining templates all returned UNSAT:

| Target order | Cycle length | Fixed points | Canonical shift sets |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 26 | 25 | 1 | 2 |
| 26 | 23 | 3 | 60 |
| 26 | 22 | 4 | 192 |
| 26 | 21 | 5 | 684 |
| 30 | 29 | 1 | 1 |
| 30 | 27 | 3 | 132 |
| 30 | 26 | 4 | 428 |
| 30 | 25 | 5 | 1268 |

A fixed set is a subquasigroup, so its size is at most one third of the
target order. Sizes 2 and 6 are excluded. The arithmetic bounds and the
23-cycle argument cover the other nonempty fixed sets. A full even-length
cycle with no fixed points is impossible already for a Latin quasigroup:
F and `F(t)−t` would both permute Z/nZ, but their sums differ by
`sum(t)=n/2`, not zero.

Together, these reductions and the finite certificates exclude **an
automorphism with exactly one nontrivial cycle** at either target order.
This does not imply that every automorphism is trivial, and it does not
exclude models with trivial automorphism group.

Searches with two or more nontrivial cyclic orbits remain unresolved. They
included two 13-cycles at order 26; two 15-cycles or six 5-cycles at order 30;
and several patterns with a fixed subquasigroup. Timeouts in those searches
are not exclusions. Positive controls for the cyclic generator reproduce
checked models at orders 7 and 8.

There is a further general restriction on possible symmetries. Every
automorphism of a Latin quasigroup of order 2 modulo 4 has at least one
odd-length cycle. To see this, the product of all row signs, all column signs,
and all symbol-permutation signs is `(-1)^(n(n−1)/2) = -1`. Signs are constant
along each automorphism orbit. If all orbits were even, each of those three
products would be +1. In particular, a fixed-point-free involution cannot
occur. The sign identity follows by comparing the two permutations taking
a cell `(i,j)` to `(j,i*j)`: first use its row and then its symbol permutation,
or first use its column and then swap coordinates.

For example, regenerate the 60 small cyclic inputs for the 23+3 case with:

```sh
python3 scripts/spectrum_63_cyclic.py 23 3 --generate-only \
  --output /tmp/e63-cyclic-23-3
```

Omit `--generate-only` to run bounded searches; `--seconds` sets the per-case
CPU limit and `--jobs` controls concurrency. Generating inputs requires
`python-sat`; searching additionally requires `cadical` on PATH.

## 6. Further restrictions on a commutative order-30 model

Let u(x) be the unique element with `u(x)*x=x`. Every left translation has
order dividing three, so its fixed-point count is a multiple of three.
Those counts sum to 30. Hence the image U of u has at most ten elements.

Every point in a subquasigroup of order 1 modulo 3 belongs to U: inside that
subquasigroup its left translation has at least one fixed point. In particular,
the union of all subquasigroups of orders 4 and 7 has size at most ten.
There can be at most one order-7 subquasigroup, since two distinct ones
intersect in at most two points by the subquasigroup bound (and an intersection
of size 2 is impossible), giving a union of size at least thirteen.

There can be at most four distinct order-4 subquasigroups. Indeed, suppose
five existed. Their union has size m≤10 and they have twenty incidences.
Any two intersect in at most one point. If r_x counts incidences at x, then

\[
  \sum_x {r_x\choose2}\le10,\qquad
  \sum_x {r_x\choose2}\ge\tfrac12(400/m-20)\ge10.
\]

Equality forces m=10, every r_x=2, and every two subquasigroups to intersect.
The local unit of a shared point lies in both subquasigroups, so a singleton
intersection {x} forces u(x)=x. Every point of each four-point subquasigroup
would then be idempotent, contradicting even-order commutative idempotence.

It follows that a commutative order-30 model with a fixed-point-free
automorphism of order 5 contains **no subquasigroup of order 4 or 7**.
Such an automorphism cannot preserve a set of either size, so any such
subquasigroup would have an orbit of five distinct subquasigroups, violating
the bounds just proved.

Another natural symmetry is excluded entirely in the commutative case:
**two 13-cycles and four fixed points**. The fixed points form an order-4
subquasigroup. Since U is invariant and has size at most ten, every local
unit must be fixed by the automorphism. Each 13-cycle therefore has a constant
local unit. Each of the four fixed rows has exactly one fixed point inside
the four-point subquasigroup, so its full fixed-point count has the form
`1+13k`, where k is the number of 13-cycles assigned to it. Divisibility by
three requires k=2 for each of the four rows, but there are only two cycles.

These are restrictions on possible commutative models, not a proof that
order 30 is absent.
